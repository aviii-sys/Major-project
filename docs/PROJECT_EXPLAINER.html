The short version

  • We are testing whether AI models can forecast the water level of a Texas river 15 minutes to 3 hours ahead, early enough to warn people before a flash flood.
  • Most of the work so far went into testing fairly: checking the data, stopping the models from cheating, and comparing them against simple methods.
  • Result: neural networks (LSTM and GRU) did not clearly beat simple methods such as ridge regression. Every model, simple or neural, reacts late to a sudden rise: measured over the 18 flood events, a 3-hour forecast shows a given level a median of about 2 to 2.5 hours after the river reached it.
  • Reason: the river gauge only finds out about heavy rain when the water arrives. Gauges further up the river often rise 6 to 26 hours earlier, in roughly a third of the floods we studied.
  • Next: bring in that upstream information, then test the Transformer-LSTM hybrid on it. We do not claim that any model gives reliable flood warnings yet.

The problem in plain words

A flash flood is a fast rise of a river after heavy rain, sometimes within minutes or hours. We study one measuring point, the Guadalupe River near Spring Branch, Texas (US Geological Survey station 08167500). It records the water height, called stage, in feet every 15 minutes. The US National Weather Service defines flood levels for this gauge.

The task: given the recent history of the stage, predict the stage 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 1 hour and 3 hours ahead. The difficulty: the gauge changes only after the rain has fallen, run off the land and travelled down the river. Nothing in the gauge's own past says that a storm has just hit upstream.

What we did, step by step

  1. Checked the data before trusting it. The first dataset we planned to use (2022 to 2023) never rose above 2.86 ft, so it contained no flood. We rebuilt the dataset for 2007 to 2026. It has 20 events above 10 ft, coming from only 16 separate storms, and 6 events at official flood levels from 5 storms. (The 10 ft level is our own experimental target, not an official flood level, and we keep the two apart.)
  2. Set strict rules for fair testing. Neighbouring 15-minute readings are almost identical. If test readings sit next to training readings, a model looks good just by remembering. So we keep whole storms together, only test on later periods than the model was trained on, and leave a gap of 27 hours 15 minutes between them.
  3. Built simple benchmarks. Persistence ("the future equals now"), a damped trend ("keep moving the way it is moving, but less"), ridge regression and XGBoost. A fancy model has to beat these to be worth its complexity.
  4. Trained neural networks. LSTM, GRU and a Transformer, using the last 24 hours of the river's own readings.
  5. Tested six times on later periods. Each time the models learn from the past and are scored on a later block they have never seen. In total 18 flood events were forecast out of sample.
  6. Asked why every model is late. We looked at rainfall data and at gauges further up the river.
  7. Tested whether more history helps. We repeated the GRU test with 72 hours of history instead of 24 hours (36 training runs). The rule for deciding was written down in advance (default: keep 24 hours). The result is in finding 6.

What we found

1. Simple methods are very hard to beat

Two things to notice. The neural networks are no better than ridge regression. And during floods the error is roughly 15 times larger than at other times, which is exactly when accuracy matters most. In the flood windows at 15 and 30 minutes the neural networks were slightly worse than the simple methods. An early test on a single split had shown the GRU ahead. That did not survive the stricter six-fold test.

2. Catching sudden rises is hard for every model

Counting how many sudden rises (at least 4 ft within 3 hours, an experimental target) each method flagged in time when forecasting 3 hours ahead. There were 25 such events.

Ridge12 of 25
Damped trend10 of 25
GRU7 of 25
LSTM4 of 25

3. Everyone is late

The reason is not a weak model. A forecast of a rise that has not yet started at the gauge cannot be made from the gauge's own history. The model sees the rise only once it begins.

4. Rainfall data we tried did not help

The common global rainfall dataset (ERA5) reported about 10 mm of rain over the basin on the day of the May 2015 flood. A network of rain gauges, combined into one basin estimate, gave about 119 mm, so ERA5 held roughly 8% of it. The gauge estimate only gives daily totals, so it cannot signal when a storm starts.

5. The missing information is often upstream

Seven gauges upstream have 15-minute records for the whole period. Over the 18 flood events, the upstream gauges rose 6 to 26 hours before Spring Branch in 6 to 8 events (the range depends on how the rise is timed; storms with several pulses are ambiguous). In another 6 to 8 events the rain fell below the upstream gauges, so they give no warning. That group includes two official floods (May 2015 and October 2015). The gauges also stopped recording during two of the biggest floods, and one important gauge has a 2.9 ft datum shift in 2011, so any model using them needs care.

What this means. The main limit is probably missing information, not model size. Adding upstream gauges could help for floods that start upstream. For floods that start close to Spring Branch, only rainfall information at the right place and time could help. We have not yet tested whether a model can use the upstream signal.

6. Three days of history instead of one did not help

We repeated the GRU test with 72 hours of the river's own history instead of 24 hours: 3 training runs each on all six test periods, 36 in total. The rule was fixed in advance: switch only if the longer history clearly improves flood-time forecasts at two or more of the four horizons. It improved none. Over all forecasts the 72 hour version was slightly worse at 15 minutes, 30 minutes and 1 hour (by about 1 to 2% of the error), it took about three times as long to train, and it showed no clear gain in catching sudden rises. So the default stays at 24 hours.

This fits the upstream finding: more of the river's own past does not say where the rain fell. It does not prove that no model could use longer history. With three training runs per setting and 14 storms in the tests, it is one more exploratory result, not a final answer.

What went wrong, and how we handled it

Finding and recording our own mistakes is part of the method. They are all written into the project documents.

  • The first dataset had no flood. We caught it in the first data check and rebuilt the dataset.
  • A data bug hid the biggest test flood. One missing reading blanked 72 hours of inputs, so the largest flood was scored as a miss for every model. We found it, fixed it, kept both versions of the results, and labelled the corrected results as exploratory.
  • Our first upstream analysis was wrong. It silently dropped three gauges and gave impossible timings. We rewrote it and kept the invalid files as evidence.
  • We overclaimed once. We said 15-minute discharge data existed for two historic floods (1997 and 2002). The peaks were missing, and we corrected the claim.

What we can and cannot claim

We can say

  • Our tests are built to avoid cheating: whole storms are kept together and tests are on later periods.
  • Simple methods are strong baselines for this gauge.
  • Neural networks showed no reliable advantage over them in this setup.
  • For some floods, upstream gauges hold hours of advance information.
  • The common global rainfall dataset was inadequate for the storms we checked.

We cannot say

  • That any model gives reliable flash-flood warnings.
  • That deep learning is better, or that Transformers are unsuitable (we tested one setup).
  • That results hold at other rivers.
  • That anything is untouched validation: the whole record was inspected, so all results are exploratory.
  • That this runs in real time. It is a study on historical data.

There are few independent events (14 storms in the out-of-sample tests, and only 5 storms at official flood levels), so all numbers carry wide uncertainty.

What is next, and what we ask the guide

  • Review the 24 hour versus 72 hour result with the guide (the pre-agreed rule says keep 24 hours).
  • Discuss adding upstream gauges as inputs before building the hybrid Transformer-LSTM, so that the hybrid starts from the best available information.
  • A bounded check of why the Transformer trained unstably (it is not evidence against Transformers).

Questions for the guide:

  1. May the central claim become: "a hybrid that also sees upstream context improves forecasting of upstream-driven floods, and we report its limits for the rest"?
  2. Is it acceptable to limit the early-warning claim to floods that have an upstream signal?
  3. Should radar rainfall (available for 16 of the 18 events by date) be validated against the gauge estimate first?
  4. Which upstream gauge first? Comfort has the best coverage.
  5. Should the hybrid be built before or after the upstream-input test? We recommend after.

How we present it to the university

Assumptions to confirm with the guide first. We assume a panel of faculty and the guide, about 15 to 20 minutes plus questions, with slides. If the slot, the panel, or the required deliverables are different (a live demo, code walk-through, written report), adapt the plan below.

The one message

"We built a strict test bench for flash-flood forecasting and found that the bottleneck is missing upstream information, not model complexity."

Three takeaways for the audience

  1. The testing is rigorous: no cheating, strict comparison with simple methods, and mistakes are recorded.
  2. The result is a clear, honest finding: neural networks did not beat simple methods, and every model is 2 to 3 hours late to sudden rises at the 3 hour horizon.
  3. The data point to the next step: upstream gauges often rise hours earlier.

Say and avoid

Say

  • "Our test is designed so models cannot cheat."
  • "Simple methods were hard to beat."
  • "The information a sudden rise needs often sits upstream."
  • "These results are exploratory."

Avoid

  • "Our AI predicts floods."
  • "Deep learning is better."
  • "This is a real-time warning system."
  • Any number you cannot point to in a project table.

The demo

There is no live dashboard yet, so do not present one. Instead, walk through one flood (the July 2025 chart): what the river did, what each model forecast, and where upstream rose first. It is more convincing than a mock-up.
